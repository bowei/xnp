Reading this

Conversion is linear in object count, at every shape. The power-law exponent lands between 0.98 and 1.07 across all nine series — no super-linear GC term emerges up to 1M live objects. Memory is linear to a fraction of a percent: resident bytes per object are flat from 4k to 1M (72.0 B for a minimal Namespace at both ends). So extrapolating to 10M is an extrapolation of slope only, which is the well-behaved kind.

Time is not the binding constraint; memory is. 10M pods convert in tens of seconds but only a few GiB resident. 10M large NetworkPolicies would be ~25 minutes of CPU and ~870 GiB — the machine runs out of RAM roughly three orders of magnitude before it runs out of patience. The benchmark enforces this itself: cases over -repr.membudget skip and report their estimate rather than driving the box into swap.

Allocated ≠ resident, and the gap is the interesting part. For Namespaces and Pods the two track each other. For a medium NetworkPolicy the batch allocates ~25.9 KB per object but retains ~9.1 KB — roughly two thirds is transient, thrown off while compiling predicates. That churn is what the collector pays for even though it never shows up in the resident figure.

Caveats. Single-threaded, one machine, one Go version; source-object generation runs with the timer stopped, so these are conversion costs only, not decode-plus-convert. The NetworkPolicy medium and large series are measured to 1e5 and 1e4 respectively — everything past that is model, and the charts dash the line where measurement stops.
